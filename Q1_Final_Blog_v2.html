Leaderboards Hide the Real Signal

Every TTS leaderboard answers who won. Almost none answer the question model builders actually need: what specific failure caused each loss. VoiceArena's blind pairwise protocol — where evaluators listen to two synthetic voices, pick a winner, and write down exactly what they heard — generates something rarer: a dense, language-by-language record of TTS failure from real human perception.

We analysed all 44,387 votes from the full VoiceArena dataset, covering nine commercial models across six languages. The analysis focuses on defect tags: structured failure labels extracted from evaluator comments. The result is a six-category taxonomy, ranked by actual frequency — not assumption.

Two findings stand out immediately. First: mispronunciation is the dominant failure globally at 39.9% of all defect mentions — not prosody, as earlier analyses assumed. Second: two categories entirely absent from prior work are clearly present here — hallucination (extra content added by the model, 7.8%) and missing words (content dropped by the model, 6.0%). These are not quality gradations. They are factual reliability failures.

"Knowing your model ranked fifth is useless. Knowing that 39.9% of negative evaluations cite mispronunciation — and that in Japanese, it's 49.3% — is something you can fix tomorrow."

The Six-Category Defect Taxonomy

Percentages below reflect each tag's share of all defect mentions across the full corpus (67,263 total tag instances from 44,387 votes; multiple tags possible per vote).

1 — Mispronunciation (mild + severe) 39.9% of defect tags
The single dominant failure across all six languages and all nine models. Evaluators flag wrong stress placement, syllable errors, phoneme substitutions, and words that become unrecognisable. Mild mispronunciation (24.1%) and severe mispronunciation (15.8%) are tracked separately — the severe category alone exceeds every other single defect type except robotic voice. Proper nouns, numbers, code-switched words (English inside Hindi text), and Arabic phonemes are the highest-frequency triggers.
2 — Unnatural / robotic voice 25.1% of defect tags
Evaluator descriptions: "robotic", "mechanical", "monotone", "flat delivery". This is a timbre and naturalness failure distinct from pacing — it describes the intrinsic vocal quality of the output, not its rhythm. It is the top failure for English (32.8%) and Vietnamese (33.2%), and OpenAI gpt-4o-mini-tts has the highest rate of any model at 36.2%.
3 — Irregular pacing / pause distribution 15.6% of defect tags
Evaluators flag pauses that break syntactic boundaries, rushed delivery at clause ends, or hesitation mid-word. English shows the sharpest pacing signal: robotic voice (32.8%) and irregular pacing (30.0%) together account for nearly two thirds of all English defects. Cartesia Sonic-3 has pacing as its top defect (24.3%) — the most model-specific signal in the dataset, suggesting an architectural rather than data-quality origin.
4 — Hallucination / extra content 7.8% of defect tags
The model synthesises words, numbers, or phrases not present in the input text. Evaluator examples: adding "thousand" to a number, inserting a word not in the prompt, generating a trailing phrase from training distribution. This is a factual reliability failure — content is fabricated. For example: input "The meeting is at 3 PM" → output "The meeting is at 3 thousand PM." The word "thousand" was never in the input. xAI Grok TTS has the highest hallucination rate: 1,323 hallucination tags, representing 11.6% of all defects flagged against it. Any evaluation with a hallucination is almost always a loss.
5 — Missing word / skipped content 6.0% of defect tags
Words dropped entirely from the output. Common triggers: function words ("not", "but"), numbers in sequences, closing punctuation phrases. Distinct from mispronunciation — the word is absent, not malformed. A factual completeness failure. For example: input "Please do not press the button" → output "Please do press the button." A single dropped function word reverses the meaning. Arabic shows the highest missing-word rate, where dropped words often alter sentence meaning.
6 — Noise / distortion / glitches 5.6% of defect tags
Clipping, static, echo, repeated syllables. These are vocoder or post-processing artefacts — hard failures unrelated to language quality. ElevenLabs Eleven v3 has the highest noise rate among top-tier models (15.0% of its defect tags), notably above the field — pointing to a pipeline or vocoder issue rather than a language-quality problem, and a different class of fix. Low frequency overall, but decisive: evaluations with artefacts are almost always lost.

Two categories that do not exist in this dataset

"Emotional register mismatch" and "accent drift" are not defect tags in the VoiceArena comment corpus. They appear in earlier analyses of this dataset but are not supported by the actual evaluator language. There is no structured signal for accent or emotion failure in these 44,387 votes. Model teams should not prioritise these as improvement targets based on VoiceArena data alone.

The language finding that most diverges from prior assumptions is Japanese. Japanese is not a prosody or emotion problem — it is a pronunciation problem. Nearly half of all negative comments for Japanese evaluations cite mispronunciation. The same pattern holds for Arabic (51.6%). Model teams targeting these languages should prioritise grapheme-to-phoneme accuracy and phoneme coverage, not expressiveness.

Japanese also has the largest win-rate gap in the dataset: 55 percentage points between the best model (Google Gemini, 63%) and the worst (Microsoft Azure, 8%). This gap is not visible in English-only benchmarks.

The Sarvam finding

Sarvam Bulbul-V3 was evaluated only in Hindi (2,473 votes), achieving a 33.3% win rate against a full international field. Its top defects — mispronunciation and robotic voice — are the same as the global pattern. For teams building India-specific voice products, this represents an early and directly comparable benchmark.

The Hard Floor: Hallucination and Missing Words

The two failure modes least visible in standard TTS evaluation pipelines are the ones with the most decisive effect on individual evaluations: hallucination and missing words.

A model that adds content — an extra number, a trailing phrase, a word not in the input — flips the meaning of the utterance. xAI Grok TTS accounts for 1,323 hallucination tags (25.3% of all hallucination tags in the dataset). A model that drops a word — especially a negation or a number in a sequence — creates a factual error. These failures are not captured by UTMOS, NISQA, or WER. They only appear in human evaluation with written feedback.

For model builders: fixing hallucinations and omissions likely yields a larger win-rate gain than polishing prosody, because any evaluation containing either failure is almost certainly a loss regardless of everything else.

Priorities for Model Builders

1
Mispronunciation is global priority #1 — especially in JA and AR
39.9% of all defect tags. In Japanese and Arabic, it exceeds 50% of language-specific defects. G2P accuracy, phoneme coverage, and native-speaker fine-tuning should precede prosody work for any team underperforming in these languages.
2
Audit for hallucination and missing words before optimising quality
These are zero-tolerance failures in any production use case. Run a targeted audit on your model's behaviour with numbers, proper nouns, and function words. xAI Grok's 11.6% hallucination rate suggests this is not an edge case.
3
For English: robotic voice is the dominant failure — not pronunciation
English has the lowest mispronunciation rate in the field (14.7%), but robotic voice is at 32.8%. OpenAI gpt-4o-mini-tts at 36.2% identifies this as its primary lever. Expressive training data and flow-matching architectures address this; marginal WER improvement does not.
4
Noise/distortion is a pipeline issue — ElevenLabs should investigate
ElevenLabs v3 has the highest noise/distortion rate among competitive models (15.0% of its defect tags). This points to a vocoder or post-processing issue rather than a language model problem — a different class of fix from prosody or pronunciation work.
5
Google Gemini 3.1 Flash TTS is the current reference — study its failures
53.8% overall win rate, winning every language pair including 63% in Japanese and 61% in Arabic. Its top defect (robotic voice at 28.4%) is still improvable — and closing that gap is where the next leaderboard shifts will come from.

Why This Taxonomy Matters More Than Automated Metrics

UTMOS predicts mean opinion score. NISQA predicts perceived quality. WER measures intelligibility. None of them capture hallucination. None of them distinguish robotic voice from irregular pacing. None of them flag a missing negation.

VoiceArena's comment corpus captures what evaluators actually hear, in their own words, tied to a specific audio comparison, at scale. The six categories in this taxonomy did not come from an a-priori framework — they emerged from the language 21,756 evaluators reached for when describing failure. That emergence is the signal.

As TTS quality converges at the top of the leaderboard — where the gap between leading models narrows and automated metrics plateau — the qualitative differences that human listeners name in real time become the only signal that reliably predicts pairwise preference. This analysis makes that signal legible from 44,387 votes.
