First — What Is an AI Voice, and Why Are We Testing It?

An AI voice (also called a TTS model — Text-To-Speech) is software that reads any written text aloud in a human-like voice. You have heard it everywhere. The Google Maps navigation voice? That is TTS. The Siri voice on iPhone? TTS. The automated voice when you call a bank's helpline? Also TTS.

Many big companies — Google, OpenAI, ElevenLabs, Microsoft, Cartesia, and others — have built their own AI voice products. Each one sounds a little different. Some sound very natural. Some sound robotic. Some handle Hindi well. Some do not.

VoiceArena is a platform that tests these AI voices against each other using real human listeners — not machines — to decide which one sounds better. Think of it like a blind taste test, except instead of food, people are listening to voices.

Simple analogy

Imagine you are a judge on a singing show like Indian Idol. Two contestants sing the same song. You listen to both — you do not know their names or backgrounds. You pick who sounded better, and you write down exactly what went wrong with the other one. VoiceArena does this same thing, except the "contestants" are AI voice products, and 44,387 people are the judges.

The Problem With Just Looking at Rankings

VoiceArena already publishes a ranking — which AI voice is number one, number two, and so on. But a ranking alone is like a cricket scorecard that only shows the final score. It tells you who won. It does not tell you why they won or what the losing team did wrong.

The more valuable information was hidden in the written comments. Nearly half the people who voted (21,756 out of 44,387) also wrote a sentence or two explaining exactly what they heard that was wrong. We read all those comments, grouped them by type, and counted how often each type of problem came up.

The result is a ranked list of the 6 most common ways AI voices go wrong. We call this the defect list — the specific mistakes that make people choose one voice over another.

Simple analogy

Think of Zomato restaurant reviews. The star rating tells you the restaurant is rated 3.2 out of 5. But it is the written reviews that tell you why — "biryani was cold", "waiter was rude", "chicken was undercooked". Those written complaints are far more useful to the restaurant owner than the star rating alone. Our analysis does the same thing for AI voices.

"Knowing your AI voice ranked 5th is useless. Knowing that 40% of the people who disliked it said it mispronounced words — and that in Japanese, nearly half of all complaints were about pronunciation — is something you can actually fix."

The 6 Most Common Ways AI Voices Go Wrong

These six categories came directly from what 21,756 real listeners wrote in their comments. Each percentage tells you how often that specific type of complaint appeared out of all the complaints collected.

1 — Said Words Wrong (Mispronunciation) 39.9% of all complaints
What this means: The AI voice said a word incorrectly. It either stressed the wrong syllable (like saying "PHOtograph" as "phoTOgraph"), substituted one sound for another, or made a word completely unrecognisable.

When it happens most: Names of people and places, technical words, and sentences that mix two languages (like an English word inside a Hindi sentence).

Why it matters: This is the single biggest complaint across all 6 languages and all 9 AI voice products. Nearly 4 in every 10 complaints were about this. It is the number one problem the AI voice industry needs to fix.
2 — Sounds Like a Robot (Unnatural/Robotic Voice) 25.1% of all complaints
What this means: The voice sounds mechanical, flat, or lifeless — like the old automated voices you hear when you call a customer care number and press 1 for English. The words are correct, but it does not feel like a real person speaking.

Where it is worst: English and Vietnamese listeners complained about this the most. One specific AI product — OpenAI's voice — had 36 out of every 100 complaints about it being robotic. That is the highest robotic rate of any product in the test.

Why it matters: People stop trusting a voice that sounds fake. Even if every word is correct, a robotic tone makes listeners uncomfortable and they will prefer the competitor.
3 — Weird Pauses (Bad Timing / Irregular Pacing) 15.6% of all complaints
What this means: The voice stops or pauses in the wrong place — like a news anchor who takes a breath in the middle of a word instead of at the end of a sentence. Or rushes through the last part of a sentence as if it ran out of time.

Example: Imagine someone reading "Please meet me at the station... tomorrow morning" but pausing after "the" instead — "Please meet me at the... station tomorrow morning." It sounds strange and breaks the listener's concentration.

Interesting finding: Cartesia's AI voice had this as its number one complaint (24 out of every 100 complaints about Cartesia were about bad timing). This is the most model-specific finding — it points to a specific design issue in that product.
4 — Made Up Words That Were Not There (Hallucination) ⚠️ 7.8% of all complaints
What this means: The AI voice added words that were not in the original text. You gave it a sentence to read, and it read that sentence — plus extra words it invented on its own.

Example: You typed "The meeting is at 3 PM." The AI read "The meeting is at 3 thousand PM" — adding the word "thousand" that was never there. Or it added a complete extra phrase at the end that you never wrote.

Why this is the most serious problem: This is not a quality issue. This is a reliability issue. If an AI voice reads a medical instruction and adds or changes words, it could cause real harm. Any listener who notices this immediately loses trust in the product — and will almost always prefer the other voice.

Worst offender: xAI's Grok voice product had the highest rate of this problem. About 11 in every 100 complaints about Grok were about it adding words that were not there.
5 — Skipped Words That Were There (Missing Words) ⚠️ 6.0% of all complaints
What this means: The AI voice skipped a word from the original sentence entirely. The word was in the text, but the voice just... did not say it.

Example: You wrote "Please do not press the button." The AI read "Please do press the button." — it dropped the word "not" completely. The sentence now means the exact opposite of what you wrote.

Why this is also very serious: Just like problem #4 above, this is not about sound quality — it is about accuracy and trust. A voice assistant that drops words cannot be used in any important situation. Arabic language listeners experienced this the most in this dataset.
6 — Crackling / Static / Audio Glitches (Noise) 5.6% of all complaints
What this means: The audio itself had a technical problem — a crackling sound, static, an echo, a syllable that repeated by mistake, or the audio cutting off suddenly. These are not language problems — they are like a bad phone connection or a microphone issue.

Interesting finding: ElevenLabs — one of the top-ranked products — had the highest rate of this problem among the competitive products (15 in every 100 ElevenLabs complaints). This suggests a technical issue in their audio processing pipeline, separate from how natural or accurate the voice sounds.

Why even a small rate is a big deal: Any evaluation where the listener hears a glitch is almost always a loss. There is no forgiveness for this. It is like a singer hitting a wrong note on the highest note of the song — it ruins everything.

Two things that were claimed but are NOT in the data

Some earlier discussions about AI voice quality mentioned "emotional mismatch" (the voice sounds emotionless) and "accent problems" (sounds like a foreigner speaking Hindi) as major categories. When we looked at the actual complaint data from 44,387 real listeners, neither of these appeared as structured complaint categories. We have not included them in this list because they are not supported by the actual data. Good research only reports what the data actually shows.

Does the Same Problem Exist in Every Language?

No — and this is one of the most important findings. Different languages have very different main problems. A product that works well in English might fail badly in Japanese. A problem that is rare in English might be very common in Arabic.

Simple analogy

Think of a chef who is excellent at making Italian food. If you ask them to make a South Indian thali, they might struggle with completely different problems — different spices, different techniques, different expectations from the people eating it. AI voice products have the same challenge across languages.

The most surprising finding here is Japanese. If you only looked at the global data, you might think "robotic voice is the biggest problem." But Japanese listeners said something very different — nearly 1 in 2 complaints about Japanese AI voices was about pronunciation. The AI voices are saying Japanese words wrong. That is a completely different problem that needs a completely different solution.

The India finding — Sarvam Bulbul-V3

Sarvam is an Indian AI company that built a voice product specifically for Hindi. In this test, it was only evaluated in Hindi (2,473 tests) and won about 1 in 3 comparisons (33.3%). Its main problem areas — pronunciation and robotic voice — are the same as the global average. For companies building voice products specifically for Indian languages, this is a direct, honest benchmark of where an India-focused product currently stands against the global competition.

The Two Most Serious Problems (Problems 4 and 5)

Problems 1, 2, and 3 above are quality problems. The voice sounds bad, feels unnatural, or has bad timing. These are annoying, but they are improvement opportunities.

Problems 4 and 5 — making up words and skipping words — are in a completely different category. These are accuracy and trust problems.

Imagine if Google Maps occasionally added or removed a turn from your directions. Not very often — maybe 1 in 12 trips. You might not even notice at first. But the moment you notice it once, you stop trusting it completely. That is exactly what hallucination and missing words do to an AI voice product.

For companies using AI voices in important settings — medical instructions, legal documents, financial information, educational content — these two problems need to be fixed before anything else. A beautiful-sounding voice that occasionally changes the meaning of sentences is worse than a slightly robotic voice that is always accurate.

What Should AI Voice Companies Do Next?

1
Fix pronunciation first — especially for Japanese and Arabic
Nearly 40% of all complaints globally are about pronunciation. In Japanese and Arabic, it is nearly 50%. Before anything else, AI voice teams should focus on making sure their product says words correctly in every language they support — not just in English.
2
Check if your product is adding or removing words — and fix it immediately
xAI Grok's hallucination rate (11.6% of its complaints) is a red flag. Every AI voice team should run a simple test: give the product 500 sentences and check if the output matches the input word-for-word. Any mismatch is a critical bug. This test takes one afternoon to run and could save the product's reputation.
3
For English: work on naturalness, not pronunciation
English is the one language where pronunciation is actually the least common complaint. The bigger problem in English is sounding robotic and having bad timing. OpenAI's product — with 36.2% of its English complaints being about sounding robotic — should focus on making the voice feel more human, not on correcting pronunciations.
4
ElevenLabs: investigate the audio quality issue
ElevenLabs is ranked second overall, but has the highest crackling/static rate among competitive products (15% of its complaints). This is a technical pipeline problem — not a language problem — and should be fixable separately from improving naturalness.
5
Do not treat all languages as the same problem
English rankings are not a good predictor of Japanese or Arabic performance. A product that scores well in English can fail badly in Japanese — and the reasons are completely different. Each language needs its own evaluation, its own training data focus, and its own improvement roadmap.

Why Listening to Real People Beats Computer Testing

There are automated computer programs that test AI voice quality — they give a score based on acoustic measurements and technical analysis. These are useful for fast testing. But they have a fundamental limitation.

A computer cannot tell you that it noticed the word "thousand" was added. A computer cannot tell you that the pause felt wrong at that specific point in the sentence. A computer cannot tell you that the Hindi voice sounds "like it was trained on non-Indian data."

Only a real human listener, writing down what they actually heard, can give you that signal. The 21,756 written comments in VoiceArena's data contain the most detailed, honest, and useful feedback on AI voice quality available anywhere. This analysis makes that feedback easy to act on — by converting 44,387 individual human opinions into six clear categories of problems, ranked by how serious they are.
